Water extracts the flavour from coffee. The minerals in it decide **how much** gets extracted and **how** it tastes afterwards.

## Hard and soft water

Hardness is about how much **calcium and magnesium** is in the water. A little is good: they help pull flavour out of the coffee. **Magnesium** tends to lift acidity and fruit, while **calcium** adds body and softens the acidity. Too much of either leaves scale in the kettle and the machine.

The key one is **bicarbonate** (alkalinity). It neutralises the coffee's acidity. A little is fine, but too much makes coffee flat and dull, because the bright, fruity acidity disappears.

Danish tap water comes from groundwater and is usually **hard**. It is clean and tastes good from the tap, but it is not ideal for light-roasted speciality coffee.

## What is good water for coffee?

Roughly, the Specialty Coffee Association recommends:

| | Target | Acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| Total dissolved solids (TDS) | 150 mg/L | 75–250 mg/L |
| Calcium hardness | 68 mg/L CaCO₃ | 17–85 mg/L |
| Alkalinity | 40 mg/L CaCO₃ | approx. 40 mg/L |
| pH | 7.0 | 6.5–7.5 |
| Chlorine | 0 | 0 |

You do not need to measure at home. The table just shows the direction: **moderate minerals, low alkalinity and no chlorine.**

## What can you do at home?

- **Use a filter jug.** It removes chlorine and some of the scale, and you can taste it.
- **Try low-mineral bottled water** if you want to experiment. Compare it with tap water on the same bean.
- **Avoid distilled water.** Without minerals the coffee tastes empty and sour.
- **Use fresh, cold water** in the kettle every time. Water boiled several times tastes flat.

## Temperature

For pour over and filter we use water between 92 and 96 °C. No thermometer? Let the kettle stand for 30 seconds after it boils.
