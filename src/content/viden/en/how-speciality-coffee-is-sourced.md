## What is speciality coffee?

**Speciality coffee** (or specialty coffee, as the Americans spell it) is coffee that scores at least 80 out of 100 when it is cupped and graded on the Specialty Coffee Association's scale. That is perhaps 5–10% of the world's coffee. The cherries are picked ripe, sorted with care, and the beans have no defects that spoil the taste.

It is also what people call **third wave** coffee. The first wave was coffee as a commodity, and the second was the chains' espresso drinks. The third wave treats coffee as a raw product, like wine, where origin, variety and processing shape the flavour.

## Traceability

Good speciality coffee can be traced back to **the country, the region and often the farm or cooperative**. That is why the beans in the coffee bar have names like *Ethiopia Hadeso* and *Guatemala Las Amapolas*: the name tells you where they come from.

Traceability is also about payment. When the roaster knows who grew the coffee, they can pay for quality and build relationships that last for years.

## Processing

The coffee bean is the seed of a coffee cherry. How the cherry is removed changes the taste a lot:

- **Washed:** the fruit is washed off before the bean dries. That gives a clear, clean and often bright cup.
- **Natural:** the whole cherry dries in the sun. That gives more sweetness, body and obvious fruit, such as berries.
- **Honey:** some of the fruit stays on while it dries. It sits somewhere in between.

The full explanation – including honey, anaerobic and decaf – is in [coffee processing](/en/knowledge/coffee-processing-washed-natural-honey).

## Coffee is seasonal

Coffee is harvested once or twice a year, at different times around the world. That is why the beans on the shelf change through the year. When a coffee is gone, it does not come back until the next harvest.
