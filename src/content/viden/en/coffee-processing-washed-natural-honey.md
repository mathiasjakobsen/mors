A coffee bean is the seed of a cherry. Before it can be roasted, everything around it has to be removed, and the bean dried from around 60% moisture down to 10–12%. **How** that is done is called processing. It is printed on almost every bag of speciality coffee, and it is worth understanding, because it changes the taste so much.

## Inside the cherry

From the outside in, a coffee cherry is made of:

1. **The skin** – red (or yellow).
2. **The pulp** – thin and sweet.
3. **The mucilage** – a sticky, sugary layer right on the bean.
4. **The parchment** – a thin, papery shell.
5. **The silver skin** – a very thin layer that mostly falls off during roasting.
6. **The bean** – usually two, lying flat side to flat side.

The sugar in the pulp and mucilage is the key: the longer it stays on, and the more it ferments, the more it shows up in the flavour.

## Washed

The cherries are **depulped**, removing the skin and pulp straight away. The beans – still covered in mucilage – then sit in tanks of water, typically for one to three days, while the mucilage ferments and loosens. Finally they are washed clean and dried on large patios or raised beds.

**In the cup:** clear, clean and precise. The acidity stands out, and you taste the bean's origin and variety very directly.

It takes a lot of water and is common in Ethiopia, Kenya, Colombia and Central America, among others. When we wrote this, both **Ethiopia Hadeso** (Guji) and **Guatemala Las Amapolas** (Huehuetenango) in the coffee bar were washed coffees.

## Natural

The oldest method: the whole cherry is laid out to dry in the sun, usually on raised beds, and turned regularly for two to four weeks. The bean dries inside the fruit, and only afterwards is the dried cherry hulled off.

**In the cup:** fruity, sweet and full-bodied – often with notes of berries, tropical fruit or wine. Done well, it is some of the most expressive coffee there is. Done badly, it tastes fermented or "funky", because the cherry rotted instead of drying.

Natural processing is widespread in Ethiopia, Brazil and Yemen. Our single origin espresso **Mono**, from Fazenda Paraíso in Brazil, is a natural.

## Honey (and pulped natural)

A step in between: the skin and pulp are removed, but **the mucilage stays on** while the bean dries. It has nothing to do with honey – the name comes from the sticky, golden surface.

Producers often distinguish between **yellow, red and black honey**. The more mucilage left on, and the slower it dries, the darker the name – and the more sweetness and body in the cup.

**In the cup:** sweet and round, with softer acidity than a washed coffee but cleaner than a natural. Honey is especially linked to Costa Rica and El Salvador; in Brazil a similar method is called *pulped natural*.

## Wet-hulled (giling basah)

A method from Indonesia, especially Sumatra. The parchment is removed while the bean is still moist, and the bean finishes drying unprotected – quick in a humid climate.

**In the cup:** earthy, spicy and heavy with low acidity. It divides opinion – many speciality roasters avoid it, others love it.

## Anaerobic, carbonic maceration and other experiments

In recent years many producers have started controlling fermentation far more precisely:

- **Anaerobic fermentation:** cherries or beans ferment in sealed tanks without oxygen, giving intense, often very fruity and winey coffees.
- **Carbonic maceration:** whole cherries ferment in tanks filled with carbon dioxide – a technique borrowed from winemaking (it is used for Beaujolais).
- **Added yeast or extended fermentation:** the producer adds selected yeast cultures or lets fermentation run for days to bring out particular flavours.

The results can be wild and beautiful, but also overwhelming. Some coffees are also **flavoured** during fermentation (*infused*, for example with fruit or spices). That is debated in speciality coffee, because the flavour no longer comes from the coffee itself. If a bag says "infused", now you know what it means.

## Decaf – a process too

Decaf is ordinary coffee with the caffeine drawn out of the **green** bean before roasting. The most common method in speciality is the **sugarcane** or **EA process**: the beans are rinsed in water and ethyl acetate, a compound made from fermented sugarcane. The caffeine dissolves, and the bean keeps most of its flavour.

Our **Nocturne Decaf** from Colombia is made this way – so you can have a good cup in the evening too.

## Drying, resting and sorting

Whatever the method, it ends the same way: the beans are dried slowly and evenly to 10–12% moisture. Afterwards they usually **rest** for a few weeks in their parchment before being hulled, sorted by size and density, and picked for defects. Only then are they shipped in sacks to the roaster.

## In short

| Method | Removed before drying | Typical in the cup |
|---|---|---|
| Washed | Skin, pulp and mucilage | Clear, clean, bright acidity |
| Honey | Skin and pulp | Sweet, round, softer acidity |
| Natural | Nothing | Fruity, full, berries and wine |
| Wet-hulled | Skin and pulp (parchment early) | Earthy, spicy, heavy |
| Anaerobic | Depends on the method | Intense, winey, experimental |

## Which should you choose?

If you like coffee that is **clear and bright**, look for *washed*. If you want **fruit and body**, try a *natural* or a *honey*.
